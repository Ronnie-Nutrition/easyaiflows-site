How to Set Up No-Code Automation for Your Church (Without Migrating Your ChMS)

By Ronnie Craig • August 18, 2026 • 6 min read

The question I get most from church office staff isn't "does this work" — it's "what do we actually have to do." Fair question. Nobody wants to hear that fixing their volunteer follow-up requires switching software, importing years of records, and retraining the whole team. So here's the honest, step-by-step version of what setting this up actually looks like.

Step 1: You Keep Your Church Management Software

Start here because it's the thing that worries people most. Whatever you use right now — Planning Center, Breeze, Church Community Builder, Realm, Tithe.ly, Subsplash — stays exactly where it is. Automation connects to it; it doesn't replace it. Your member records, giving history, and existing processes inside that tool don't move.

This matters because a ChMS migration is a massive project on its own — data mapping, staff retraining, months of overlap. Almost nothing about fixing volunteer follow-up or visitor retention actually requires that project. The automation sits on top of what you have and connects the pieces that currently don't talk to each other.

Step 2: Pick One Workflow, Not Five

The biggest mistake I see is churches trying to automate everything in the first pass. List every place a task currently depends on someone remembering to do it by hand — visitor follow-up, volunteer reminders, event announcements, giving receipts, prayer routing. Then pick the one where a missed step costs you the most.

For most churches, that's visitor follow-up. A first-time visitor usually decides whether they're coming back before staff have had a real conversation with them, and it's the flow most churches are currently running on memory. Start there.

Step 3: Map the Actual Decisions, Not the Software

Before any workflow gets built, someone has to decide what a first-time visitor should hear on day one versus day three, or what counts as "a volunteer who's gone quiet." Those are ministry decisions, not technical ones, and getting them right the first time matters more than the tooling. This is what a strategy call is actually for — mapping the decisions, not selecting software features.

Step 4: The Build

Once the workflow is mapped, it gets assembled from connected steps — a trigger in your ChMS (a connection card, a volunteer signup), a condition, and a set of actions (a text, an email, a staff notification). This is the "no-code" part: every step is visible and readable, not hidden inside custom code. Nothing here requires touching your ChMS's underlying data structure.

Step 5: Your Staff Tests It, Then Owns It

Before it goes live for real visitors or volunteers, it gets tested with a throwaway signup to confirm the timing and wording feel right. After that, it's yours — whoever handles communications can open the flow, edit the wording, adjust timing, or pause a step without calling anyone. You're not renting access to something only we can touch.

The whole point of doing it this way: nothing about setting this up requires you to rebuild your church's technology stack. It requires deciding what should happen, once, and then letting the system carry it out the same way every time after that.

What Trips Churches Up

The most common stumbling block isn't technical — it's trying to automate a decision that actually needs to be made differently every time, like a pastoral response to a hard situation. Those stay with a person. The flows that work best are the ones that are genuinely repetitive: the same confirmation, the same reminder timing, the same announcement distribution, every time.
